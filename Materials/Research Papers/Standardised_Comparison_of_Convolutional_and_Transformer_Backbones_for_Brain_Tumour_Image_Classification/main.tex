\begin{abstract}
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the standard investigation for brain tumours, but published deep learning accuracies are difficult to compare because studies differ in data, splitting and evaluation, and because near-duplicate images from shared public sources leak between training and test partitions. The contribution to artificial intelligence is a controlled evaluation protocol: label-aware perceptual-hash deduplication before splitting, a group-aware split into five cross-validation folds and a held-out test set, an equal number of optimiser steps for every model, temperature scaling, fold ensembles with bootstrap confidence intervals and Holm-corrected McNemar tests, and attention and background-only shortcut audits. The engineering application is the selection of a deployable tumour detector and subtype classifier. Nine ImageNet-pretrained convolutional, transformer and hybrid backbones were benchmarked on binary detection and three-way subtype classification. Deduplication reduced the binary detection pool from 3,253 to 1,185 images and the subtype classification pool from 5,828 to 4,730. On 171 binary detection test images, CoAtNet-0, EfficientNetB0 and ViT-B/16 reached 99.42\% accuracy (95\% confidence interval 98.25--100\%), and no pair of models differed significantly. The subtype classification grouping left only 45 test images, on which six models were error-free, so that test set cannot rank the models. Models retrained with the head masked out still exceeded the majority-class rate for 7 of 9 binary detection and all 9 subtype classification backbones, reaching 83.04\% and 97.78\% accuracy, so these public collections carry label information outside the brain. EfficientNetB0 matched the binary detection leaders with 4.01 million parameters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Brain tumours constitute the abnormal proliferation of cells either in the brain or the central nervous system, and they are classified by their cell of origin and molecular features \citep{louis2021}. According to the Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States, 453,623 primary brain and other central nervous system tumours were diagnosed in the United States between 2016 and 2020, an average of approximately 90,700 cases per year \citep{ostrom2023}. Three major forms of primary brain tumours are studied in this paper: (i) glioma, which arises from glial cells and accounts for 26.3\% of all primary brain and other central nervous system tumours in the same registry \citep{ostrom2023}; (ii) meningioma, which develops in the meningeal layers that cover the brain and is usually benign \citep{ostrom2023}; and (iii) pituitary tumours, which grow in the pituitary gland \citep{bauer2013}.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the main non-invasive technique for brain tumour detection and characterisation \citep{villanueva2017}. Compared with computed tomography, MRI gives better soft tissue contrast without ionising radiation, and it acquires images with different types of contrast, called sequences, such as T1-weighted, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted, T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), which together show tumour location, extent, margins and surrounding oedema \citep{villanueva2017}. An MRI study is a stack of two-dimensional images called slices. Reading these studies by hand is time-consuming and subject to variability between and within readers \citep{bauer2013}, which motivates automated analysis.

Deep learning is a family of machine learning methods that learn layered representations of data directly from raw inputs \citep{lecun2015}, and it has transformed medical image analysis over the last decade \citep{litjens2017}. Its main tool for images is the convolutional neural network (CNN), which slides small learned filters over an image to detect local patterns and stacks such layers to build increasingly abstract features \citep{lecun1998}. CNN architectures such as ResNet \citep{he2016}, VGG \citep{simonyan2015}, DenseNet \citep{huang2017}, Inception \citep{szegedy2016}, EfficientNet \citep{tan2019} and MobileNet \citep{howard2019} set the benchmarks of natural image classification. The transformer, introduced for natural language processing, instead relates every part of its input to every other part through a mechanism called self-attention \citep{vaswani2017}. Vision transformers such as ViT \citep{dosovitskiy2021} and the Swin Transformer \citep{liu2021}, and hybrids such as CoAtNet \citep{dai2021}, apply this mechanism to images, which may help to relate a tumour to the surrounding anatomy \citep{khan2022,shamshad2023}. All of these networks are usually pretrained on ImageNet, a collection of more than a million labelled natural photographs \citep{deng2009,russakovsky2015}, and then fine-tuned on the target task; the pretrained network without its final classification layer is called a backbone.

Although many studies report high accuracy for individual architectures, few compare several architectural families under the same experimental settings. Each study uses its own data, preprocessing, augmentation and evaluation, so it is impossible to tell whether an accuracy of 99\% on a small, homogeneous collection is better than 97\% on a larger, heterogeneous one. Comparisons are further distorted by data leakage, in which information from the test images reaches the model during training \citep{kapoor2023}. Public brain tumour collections are frequently assembled from shared sources and carry no patient information, so duplicates must be removed before the data are split \citep{fateh2026}; otherwise the same image, or a near-identical copy, can appear in both the training and the test partition, a problem documented for combined public chest imaging collections \citep{roberts2021}. A model that has seen an image during training is not tested on new data when it meets that image again, and its reported accuracy overstates its ability to generalise. Hashing can find near-identical copies, but it cannot find adjacent slices of the same patient, which look different but are not statistically independent. A further risk is shortcut learning, in which a model bases its prediction on a spurious cue, such as the image border or the scanner, rather than on the pathology \citep{geirhos2020,zech2018,degrave2021}.

Reliable automated detection could support faster preliminary assessment, more consistent interpretation and relief of radiologist workload \citep{beam2018,defauw2018}. This paper studies two tasks with the same pipeline. \emph{Binary detection} decides whether an MRI slice contains a tumour, and \emph{subtype classification} assigns a tumour-positive slice to glioma, meningioma or pituitary tumour. The two tasks are kept separate because subtype classification is only meaningful once a tumour-positive decision has been taken.

This paper makes four contributions. First, it trains and evaluates nine ImageNet-pretrained backbones from three architectural families with one code path, on identical splits and with an identical number of optimiser steps, so that the only difference between the rows of each results table is the architecture. Second, it controls leakage before any split is drawn, with label-aware perceptual-hash deduplication and a group-aware split whose leakage audit finds no test image near a development image. Third, it replaces single-run leaderboards with five-fold cross-validation, fold-ensemble test predictions, bootstrap confidence intervals, exact McNemar tests with Holm correction, calibration analysis and measured latency and throughput. Fourth, it audits every model for reliance on image regions outside the head, through attention maps and by retraining each model on background-only images.

Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews existing studies, Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data, deduplication, splitting, models, training and evaluation, Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. The supplementary material, provided as a separate document whose figures and tables are numbered F1, F2, \dots\ and T1, T2, \dots, contains the sample images and acquisition notes (Section~\ref{sec:supp-data}); the deduplication threshold sweep, the cross-label pairs, the group and leakage audit and the augmentation examples (Section~\ref{sec:supp-curation}); the complete training settings and evaluation procedures (Section~\ref{sec:supp-training}); the training curves and per-run results of every fold model (Section~\ref{sec:supp-cv}); the binary detection result table, confusion matrices, receiver operating characteristic curves, McNemar tests and distance analysis of both tasks (Section~\ref{sec:supp-test}); the reliability diagrams, the summary efficiency table and the accuracy--latency plots (Section~\ref{sec:supp-calib}); and the per-class attention table, attention, occlusion and Grad-CAM maps of every model, and the masking and shortcut test results (Section~\ref{sec:supp-interp}).

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

Early work on automated brain tumour recognition combined hand-engineered features with machine learning classifiers. Such work used texture descriptors such as the grey level co-occurrence matrix and local binary patterns, wavelet transforms, shape features, and classifiers such as the support vector machine (SVM), random forests and $k$-nearest neighbours \citep{bauer2013,gordillo2013}. \citet{zacharaki2009} obtained around 85\% accuracy using texture and shape features and an SVM for tumour type recognition, and \citet{cheng2015} reached 90\% accuracy with intensity, texture and shape features and an SVM on 3,064 MRI slices. Hand-engineered features, however, must be redesigned for each problem and generalise poorly across datasets \citep{litjens2017}.

The success of AlexNet in 2012 \citep{krizhevsky2017} brought deep learning to medical imaging \citep{litjens2017}. \citet{he2016} introduced residual connections, which let very deep networks be trained by adding each block's input to its output. Using a pretrained ResNet50, \citet{talo2019} reported 95.23\% accuracy on 253 brain MRI images and \citet{seetha2018} reported 97.5\%. \citet{simonyan2015} showed that depth built from small $3\times 3$ convolutional filters improves accuracy; their VGG16 has about 138 million parameters, and \citet{khan2020} reported 98.69\% accuracy with VGG16 features on 3,064 slices. \citet{huang2017} connected each layer of a dense block to all preceding layers, and \citet{noreen2020} reported 99.51\% with a DenseNet121-based model. \citet{szegedy2016} used parallel convolutions of different kernel sizes to learn multi-scale features, and InceptionV3 reached 97.5\% in the subtype classification study of \citet{deepak2019} and 99.34\% in that of \citet{rehman2020}. \citet{tan2019} scaled network depth, width and input resolution together from a baseline found by neural architecture search, obtaining better accuracy with far fewer parameters, and \citet{howard2019} designed lightweight networks for mobile devices.

\citet{dosovitskiy2021} adapted the transformer to images by treating an image as a sequence of $16\times 16$ pixel patches; their ViT matched CNNs on ImageNet when pretrained on very large datasets but lacks the built-in locality of convolution, which makes it data-hungry. \citet{liu2021} restricted attention to shifted local windows in a hierarchy of scales, so that cost grows linearly with image size. \citet{dai2021} stacked convolution stages followed by attention stages in the hybrid CoAtNet, combining the inductive bias of convolution with the global receptive field of attention. \citet{shamshad2023} survey the adoption of transformers across medical imaging. For brain tumour MRI, \citet{tummala2022} reported 98.7\% test accuracy for subtype classification with an ensemble of four ViT variants on the 3,064-slice Cheng collection, and \citet{asiri2024} reported 97\% for a Swin Transformer on a 2,870-image collection.

The reliability of these benchmarks has itself come under scrutiny. \citet{kapoor2023} documented data leakage as a recurring cause of over-optimistic results in machine-learning-based science across 17 fields. \citet{fateh2026}, curating a widely used Kaggle aggregate of the Cheng, Bhuvaji and Br35H collections into the BRISC dataset, removed exact and near-duplicates before splitting to avoid leakage, and noted that the original release records no patient, sequence or slice information. In chest radiography, models have been shown to exploit hospital-specific markers and image borders instead of pathology \citep{zech2018,degrave2021}. Table~\ref{tab:existing-studies} summarises the brain tumour studies above.

Several gaps remain. Most studies compare no more than two or three models, transformers are under-represented, and splits, preprocessing and evaluation differ between studies. Few studies remove duplicate and near-duplicate images from combined collections, and source code and exact splits are rarely released. Results are commonly quoted from a single run without confidence intervals, significance tests, per-class sensitivity and specificity, calibration or measured inference cost, and whether a model relies on the anatomy or on the framing of the image is rarely tested. This paper addresses each of these gaps.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Deduplication}
\label{sec:preproc}

\subsubsection{Brain cropping}
\label{sec:crop}

Every image is converted to greyscale, smoothed with a $5\times 5$ Gaussian filter to suppress noise, and thresholded at intensity 45 to separate the head from the dark background; the outer contours of the resulting binary mask are traced by border following \citep{suzuki1985}, and the image is cropped to the bounding box of the largest one. This is the crop applied by the web application that deploys the models, so training, evaluation and deployment see the same framing. The crops are cached once at $320\times 320$ pixels and resized to the input size of each backbone; the median share of the image that the crop keeps is given for each source in Table~\ref{tab:provenance}.

\subsubsection{Label-aware deduplication}
\label{sec:dedup}

A perceptual hash summarises the visual content of an image in a short binary code, so that visually similar images receive similar codes even after resizing or recompression \citep{monga2006}; the difference between two codes is measured by the Hamming distance, the number of bit positions in which they differ \citep{hamming1950}. A 64-bit perceptual hash and a $64\times 64$ thumbnail were computed from every original image, two images within Hamming distance 5 were treated as duplicates, and within each label all but one image of each duplicate set were removed. Pairs within this distance that carry different labels were compared with the normalised cross-correlation (NCC) of their thumbnails, the correlation of their mean-subtracted pixel intensities, which lies between $-1$ and 1 and reaches 1 only for images that differ at most in brightness and contrast \citep{zitova2003}: at $\mathrm{NCC}\geq 0.98$ the pair is the same image filed under two labels and both copies were excluded as label noise, and below that the pair is a hash false positive and both were kept. As Table~\ref{tab:dedup} and Figure~\ref{fig:dedup} show, the pass removed 63.57\% of the binary detection pool, which contained no cross-label pair, and 18.84\% of the subtype classification pool, where 8 of 31 cross-label pairs were label noise; the threshold sweep (\supp{tab:sweep}), the cross-label pairs (\supp{fig:crosslabel}) and the per-source breakdown (Section~\ref{sec:supp-curation}) are in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Group-Aware Splitting}
\label{sec:split}

Cross-validation estimates how well a model generalises by training it repeatedly on part of the data and evaluating it on the rest \citep{stone1974}; here the development images are divided into five folds, and each fold model is trained on four folds and validated on the fifth, while a separate held-out test set is never used for training or model selection. Because deduplication does not remove correlated but distinct images such as adjacent slices, kept images within Hamming distance 8 of each other or with thumbnail $\mathrm{NCC}\geq 0.98$ were first joined into groups (connected components of the similarity graph), a proxy for patient-level grouping, and stratified group $k$-fold sampling \citep{pedregosa2011}, which keeps every group in one partition while preserving class proportions as far as possible, drew a test set of about 15\% of the images and the five development folds. A leakage audit then found no test image within Hamming distance 5 or 8 or at $\mathrm{NCC}\geq 0.98$ of any development image in either task (\supp{fig:groups}). The binary detection split is balanced (Table~\ref{tab:splits}), but one subtype classification group held 1,431 images, and because a group cannot be divided, the subtype classification development folds range from 154 to 2,216 images and the held-out test set holds only 45 images, a limitation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:multiclass-results}.

\subsection{Model Development}
\label{sec:models}

Nine architectures from three families were built from one model registry, so that a model name means the same implementation and pretrained checkpoint in both tasks. Eight are \texttt{torchvision} models of PyTorch \citep{paszke2019} loaded with their ImageNet weights \citep{deng2009,russakovsky2015}, and CoAtNet-0 is the \texttt{timm} model \texttt{coatnet\_0\_rw\_224}, since \texttt{torchvision} has no CoAtNet. In every model the final linear layer is replaced by one with one output for binary detection or three for subtype classification, and the auxiliary classifier of InceptionV3 is removed so that no model receives an extra loss term. The six CNNs are ResNet50, a 50-layer residual network whose bottleneck blocks add their input to their output through skip connections \citep{he2016}; VGG16, 13 convolutional layers of $3\times 3$ filters followed by three fully connected layers \citep{simonyan2015}; DenseNet121, in which each layer of a dense block receives the concatenated feature maps of all preceding layers \citep{huang2017}; EfficientNetB0, the baseline network found by neural architecture search from which the EfficientNet family is obtained by compound scaling \citep{tan2019}; MobileNetV3-Large, built from inverted residual blocks with depth-wise separable convolutions \citep{sandler2018} and tuned for mobile devices by architecture search \citep{howard2019}; and InceptionV3, which runs factorised convolutions of different sizes in parallel branches and takes $299\times 299$ inputs \citep{szegedy2016}. The two transformers are ViT-B/16, which splits the image into $16\times 16$ patches and processes them as a token sequence with a 12-layer transformer encoder \citep{dosovitskiy2021}, and Swin-Base, which computes self-attention inside shifted local windows of a four-stage hierarchy \citep{liu2021}. The hybrid CoAtNet-0 uses depth-wise convolution in its early stages and relative self-attention in its later stages \citep{dai2021}. The parameter count of every model, measured on the instantiated network, is reported with its measured cost in Section~\ref{sec:efficiency}.

\subsection{Training Configuration and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:training}

All runs used two NVIDIA Tesla T4 graphics processing units on Kaggle with PyTorch 2.11.0 \citep{paszke2019} and identical settings for every model and both tasks (\supp{tab:hyperparams}): crops normalised with the ImageNet channel means and standard deviations, training augmentation by horizontal flipping, rotation of up to $\pm 10^{\circ}$ and brightness and contrast jitter of up to 20\% (\supp{fig:augmentation}), 20 epochs with an effective batch of 64 images made up by gradient accumulation where a model did not fit on one GPU, so that every model took the same number of optimiser steps on a given fold, the AdamW optimiser \citep{loshchilov2019} with learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$ and weight decay 0.01, a one-epoch linear warm-up followed by cosine decay \citep{loshchilov2017}, half-precision training \citep{micikevicius2018}, gradient clipping at norm 1.0 \citep{pascanu2013}, binary cross-entropy for binary detection and cross-entropy for subtype classification, and, after each epoch, retention of the checkpoint with the lowest validation log-loss, a strictly proper scoring rule \citep{gneiting2007}; each backbone was also trained once on fold 0 with the head set to zero for the shortcut test, giving 54 runs per task, all of which completed (details in Section~\ref{sec:supp-training}).

\textbf{Calibration and thresholds.} For every run a temperature that rescales the model's outputs was fitted on its own validation fold by minimising the negative log-likelihood \citep{guo2017}, and for binary detection a decision threshold was chosen there by Youden's J statistic, the threshold that maximises sensitivity plus specificity minus one \citep{youden1950}.

\textbf{Cross-validation and fold ensembles.} The five fold models of each backbone were scored individually on the held-out test set and also averaged into one fold-ensemble prediction per test image, whose accuracy, macro F1 and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) received percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals from 2,000 resamples of the test images \citep{efron1979}.

\textbf{Statistical comparison.} Every pair of fold ensembles was compared with an exact McNemar test on the images that exactly one of the two misclassifies \citep{mcnemar1947}, with p-values adjusted for the 36 comparisons by the Holm procedure \citep{holm1979}.

\textbf{Attention and shortcut analysis.} For every backbone, gradient-weighted class activation maps (Grad-CAM) \citep{selvaraju2017}, attention rollout for ViT-B/16 \citep{abnar2020}, occlusion sensitivity \citep{zeiler2014}, inference with the head or the background masked out by an Otsu head mask \citep{otsu1979}, and retraining on background-only images \citep{geirhos2020} measured how much each model relies on the head, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:interpretability}.

\textbf{Efficiency.} Parameters, giga floating-point operations (GFLOPs) per image, single-image latency, batched throughput and peak training memory were measured for every model on one T4 under half-precision autocasting.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

A model is well calibrated when its predicted probabilities match observed frequencies, so that, for example, 90\% of the images it scores at 0.9 belong to the predicted class \citep{guo2017}; the expected calibration error (ECE) measures the gap by sorting predictions into 15 equal-width confidence bins and averaging the difference between confidence and accuracy over the bins, weighted by their size \citep{naeini2015}, and temperature scaling divides the model's outputs by a single fitted constant $T$ to correct over- or under-confidence without changing any prediction \citep{guo2017}. Table~\ref{tab:calibration} gives the ECE of the fold ensembles before and after temperature scaling. Every model is reasonably calibrated, with ECE between 0.0146 and 0.0326 in binary detection and between 0.0044 and 0.0371 in subtype classification, but temperature scaling lowered the ECE only for ResNet50 and ViT-B/16 in binary detection and for CoAtNet-0 in subtype classification, and raised it for every other model. When a validation fold is classified almost perfectly, minimising its negative log-likelihood drives the temperature towards the lower bound of the search, $e^{-3}\approx 0.0498$, which sharpens the test probabilities: in binary detection fold 4 the fitted temperature of six models lies between 0.0498 and 0.0682, and for five of them that fold has the highest test log-loss of the model's five folds (\supp{tab:runs-binary}). Validation folds of a few hundred near-separable images are therefore too small to fit a temperature reliably; reliability diagrams of every model are given in \supp{fig:reliability}.

\subsection{Model Efficiency Comparison}
\label{sec:efficiency}

Efficiency is measured by the number of learned parameters, the GFLOPs needed to classify one image, the single-image latency (time from input to prediction), the throughput (images classified per second when 32 are processed together), the training throughput and the peak GPU memory used in training. Table~\ref{tab:efficiency-full} gives the complete measurements. Cost varies by more than two orders of magnitude, from 0.43 GFLOPs for MobileNetV3-Large to 30.9 GFLOPs for Swin-Base and VGG16 (\supp{tab:efficiency}), while binary detection test accuracy varies by 2.34 percentage points and the differences are not significant. MobileNetV3-Large has the highest batched throughput, 2,301 images per second in binary detection, and EfficientNetB0 follows at 1,393 images per second with an accuracy equal to the leaders; Swin-Base has the lowest throughput at 198 images per second and the highest single-image latency at 36.3~ms. Single-image latency does not follow model size: VGG16, the largest model, has the lowest latency at 7.7~ms in binary detection, whereas DenseNet121 needs 23.6~ms despite its 6.95 million parameters, so neither parameter count nor GFLOPs predicts single-image latency on this hardware. Accuracy is plotted against latency in \supp{fig:latency}.

\subsection{Interpretability and Shortcut Analysis}
\label{sec:interpretability}

Interpretability analysis asks which parts of an image a model uses, and shortcut analysis asks whether it can succeed with cues unrelated to the pathology \citep{geirhos2020}. Every measurement below uses a head mask computed for each test image by Otsu thresholding \citep{otsu1979}, morphological closing, selection of the largest connected component and hole filling, and Table~\ref{tab:attention} summarises them.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Grad-CAM energy outside the head.} Grad-CAM weights the feature maps of a network's last spatial stage by the gradient of the predicted class score and sums them into a heat map of the regions that raised that score \citep{selvaraju2017}; the metric is the share of this map's total intensity (energy) that falls outside the head mask, compared with the share a uniform map would give, which equals the share of the image outside the head. The share is below the uniform value for most models and classes. ViT-B/16 is the exception in binary detection, with 42.6\% of its Grad-CAM energy outside the head on tumour-negative images against 35.6\% for a uniform map, and CoAtNet-0 places 41.7\% outside the head on the single glioma test image (\supp{tab:energy}, \supp{fig:energy}).
\item \textbf{Attention rollout.} Attention rollout multiplies the attention matrices of all transformer layers to trace how much each image patch contributes to the final prediction \citep{abnar2020}; for ViT-B/16 it places 42.1\% of its weight outside the head on binary detection tumour-negative images, agreeing with Grad-CAM.
\item \textbf{Occlusion sensitivity.} Occlusion sensitivity replaces a $32\times 32$ patch, moved with stride 16, by the image mean and records the drop in the predicted-class probability \citep{zeiler2014}; because it needs no access to the network's internals, it is computed the same way for every architecture on a common stratified sample of test images, and its share outside the head ranges from 0.7\% (VGG16) to 19.4\% (EfficientNetB0) in binary detection and from 6.6\% to 12.4\% in subtype classification (\supp{fig:occlusion-binary} and \ref{fig:occlusion-multiclass}).
\item \textbf{Head-only and background-only accuracy.} The masking test applies each fold model, unchanged, to test images whose background is set to zero (head-only) or whose head is set to zero (background-only), and reports the mean accuracy over folds; a model that depends on the head should keep its accuracy on head-only images and fall to chance on background-only images.
\item \textbf{Shortcut accuracy.} The shortcut test retrains each backbone on background-only images and evaluates it on background-only test images; its accuracy is compared with the majority-class rate, the accuracy of always predicting the most frequent class, by a one-sided exact binomial test with Holm correction \citep{holm1979}, and accuracy significantly above that rate shows that the background carries label information a model can learn \citep{geirhos2020}.
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Masking test.} The masking test (\supp{fig:masking}) shows that models trained on full images depend on the head. With the background set to zero, every model keeps most of its accuracy, between 93.7\% and 96.8\% in binary detection and between 94.2\% and 99.6\% in subtype classification. With the head set to zero, binary detection accuracy falls to between 44.4\% and 53.3\% for every model except InceptionV3 (65.7\%), close to or below the majority-class rate of 62.6\%, and subtype classification accuracy falls to between 11.6\% and 33.8\%, below its majority-class rate of 68.9\%.
\item \textbf{Shortcut test.} The shortcut test shows that this is not the whole story. When each backbone is retrained on background-only images, seven of the nine binary detection models exceed the majority-class rate after Holm correction, reaching 83.0\% for Swin-Base, 81.3\% for ResNet50 and 77.8\% for EfficientNetB0, while MobileNetV3-Large (67.8\%) and ViT-B/16 (65.5\%) are not significant; in subtype classification all nine are significant, with background-only accuracies between 82.2\% and 97.8\% (\supp{tab:shortcut}, \supp{fig:shortcut}). The background, borders and framing of these public collections therefore carry substantial label information, in the subtype classification pool almost enough to solve the test set without any brain. The models trained on full images do not need the background to perform well, but the data do not prevent them from using it, and the accuracies reported here cannot be taken as proof that pathology has been learned.
\end{itemize}

Three conclusions about architecture can be drawn from these experiments, and one cannot. First, under an equal optimisation budget every backbone family reaches 97\% to 99\% binary detection accuracy, and the differences between families are not significant on 171 test images, so this benchmark gives no support to the claim that transformers, hybrids or convolutional networks are better suited to the task. Second, cost differs by two orders of magnitude for statistically indistinguishable accuracy, which makes cost the deciding factor in practice: EfficientNetB0 matches the leaders with 4.01 million parameters and 0.77 GFLOPs and has the second-highest throughput, which is why it is the architecture deployed in the accompanying application. Third, cross-validation spread is as large as the gaps between models, so single-run leaderboards on collections of this size cannot rank architectures. What cannot be concluded is that any of these models has learned tumour morphology rather than properties of the source collections, since background-only models reach 83\% binary detection and 98\% subtype classification accuracy.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study benchmarked nine ImageNet-pretrained backbones for binary detection and subtype classification of brain tumours with one pipeline: label-aware perceptual-hash deduplication and group-aware splitting before any training, an equal number of optimiser steps for every model, five-fold cross-validation with fold ensembles, bootstrap confidence intervals and Holm-corrected McNemar tests, calibration analysis, measured efficiency, and attention and shortcut audits for every model. Deduplication removed 63.57\% of the binary detection and 18.84\% of the subtype classification images, and the leakage audit found no test image near a development image. On 171 binary detection test images, CoAtNet-0, EfficientNetB0 and ViT-B/16 reached 99.42\% accuracy, and no model differed significantly from any other. The subtype classification split was dominated by one group of 1,431 images, so its test set of 45 images cannot rank the models. Temperature scaling fitted on near-separable validation folds made calibration worse for most models. Most importantly, models retrained on background-only images exceeded chance for 7 of 9 binary detection and all 9 subtype classification backbones, with up to 83.04\% and 97.78\% accuracy, so the public collections used here and in much of the literature carry label information outside the brain, and high accuracy on them does not establish that pathology has been learned.

The principal limitations are that the images are public two-dimensional slices of unknown MRI sequence without patient identifiers, so grouping by image similarity is only a proxy for a patient-level split; that the binary detection negative class is tumour-negative rather than certified normal; that no external validation set was available; and that every model was trained once per fold with one seed and one shared configuration. Future work should split by patient where identifiers exist, use a smaller grouping tolerance or a different grouping criterion for the subtype classification pool so that its test set represents every class, remove or standardise the image border before training and repeat the shortcut test, fit calibration on larger held-out sets, and validate externally on multi-sequence and three-dimensional institutional data before any clinical claim is made.
